# Denver's inventoried ash, counted tree by tree

**One-line finding:** Denver's park and street tree inventory records 33,479 ash trees (genus Fraxinus) among 305,295 inventoried trees, 11.0 percent; in Marston, 29.6 percent of inventoried trees are ash (815 of 2,757), and in Indian Creek, 27.6 percent (924 of 3,349).

Source: City and County of Denver open data, park and street tree inventory (ODC_PARK_TREEINVENTORY_P), 359,263 records pulled October 23, 2026. Emerald ash borer (EAB) was confirmed in Denver on June 17, 2025, by the Office of the City Forester and Colorado State University experts, according to the city's June 2025 announcement. EAB kills ash trees (genus Fraxinus); it does not attack other genera that carry "ash" in a common name, such as mountain ash.

## Finding 1: One record in seven is not a tree

The inventory's 359,263 records include 53,968 records (15.0 percent) that are not an identified tree. Of those, 53,016 carry a species field naming a site state, not a species: 26,244 vacant plantable sites (including 22 marked "vacant site - new"), 10,563 vacant sites marked not plantable, 12,797 stumps (8,171 plantable, 4,626 not plantable), 3,026 sites where a tree was requested, 385 unknown, and 1 record marked "_Ash on property" (which carries tree attributes and may be a real ash tree; reclassifying it would change the ash count by one and no printed share). A further 947 records carry the species value "ResCon" (419) or "ResCon Comp" (528), administrative placeholders connected to residential construction rather than an identified species, and 5 records carry a blank species field. Every share in this brief is computed on the remaining 305,295 tree records, not on the full record count. On the full record count, ash is 9.3 percent; on the tree count, it is 11.0 percent. The denominator is stated wherever a share appears.

## Finding 2: 33,479 ash trees, and the count checks against the city's own statement

Ash is defined here by genus, and two independent fields agree exactly: 33,479 records carry a common name beginning "Ash," and 33,479 records carry a botanical name in genus Fraxinus. Green ash (Fraxinus pennsylvanica) accounts for 23,244 and white ash (Fraxinus americana) for 10,123; the remaining 112 are other Fraxinus species or unspecified Fraxinus. A name search alone would overcount: 1,154 records contain "ash" in the common name but are not Fraxinus (mountain ash, Washington hawthorn, and others) and are excluded, because EAB does not attack them.

The city's June 2025 announcement states that about 1 in 10 trees on public land in Denver is an ash, and about 1 in 6 trees citywide (an estimated 330,000 ash trees across all land, public and private). The inventory, which covers trees managed by the Department of Parks and Recreation in parks, medians, parkways, and along streets, records 11.0 percent ash, consistent with the city's public-land figure. The inventory is not a census of all Denver trees: private-yard trees outside the managed inventory are not in it, so this brief makes no claim about the citywide 1-in-6 figure.

By species, the most common inventoried tree is honey locust (23,780 trees, 7.8 percent), followed by green ash (23,244, 7.6 percent). Counted as one genus, ash is the largest single group in the inventory.

## Finding 3: Ash exposure is concentrated by neighborhood

Ash share of inventoried trees, highest neighborhoods (each with at least 100 inventoried trees; counts printed with every rate):

- Marston: 29.6 percent (815 ash of 2,757 trees)
- Indian Creek: 27.6 percent (924 of 3,349)
- Windsor: 22.7 percent (408 of 1,796)
- City Park West: 21.6 percent (486 of 2,246)
- Congress Park: 18.5 percent (1,068 of 5,781)

The largest ash counts sit in different neighborhoods: Gateway - Green Valley Ranch holds 2,374 ash trees (12.8 percent of its 18,544 inventoried trees), Central Park 1,961 (8.5 percent of 23,032), and South Park Hill 1,203 (14.4 percent of 8,360). At the low end, Union Station records 3.1 percent ash (69 of 2,197), the Central Business District 3.7 percent (52 of 1,400), and Chaffee Park 4.4 percent (77 of 1,756). The inventory also carries three pseudo-neighborhood values (Unassigned: 4,282 trees and 32 ash; N/A: 170 trees and 6 ash; blank: 12 trees and 1 ash); those records are counted citywide and excluded from the rankings above.

Among records whose site designation is "Park," the location with the most ash is City Park (400 ash trees), followed by Wallace (George M) Park (195, with a further 84 under a second, truncated location value for the park's north section) and Sloan's Lake Park (132). The City Park figure is the park grounds; the City Park neighborhood, a different boundary that also counts street trees on the surrounding blocks, records 790 ash among its 7,722 inventoried trees. Location names in the source are truncated at 28 characters, so park counts are counts of each recorded location value, as filed.

## Finding 4: Most inventoried ash is street trees maintained by the adjacent property owner, not park trees

Of the 33,479 ash trees, 22,574 (67.4 percent) carry the site designation "Private Maintained Street Tr" (the source value is truncated; it denotes street trees maintained by the adjacent private owner). Park-designated ash number 2,612 (7.8 percent), school sites 1,508 (4.5 percent), and street trees under the plain "Street Tree" designation 1,463 (4.4 percent); 1,956 ash records (5.8 percent) carry a blank site designation. Which party maintains a tree differs by designation in the source data; the treatment and replacement programs described on the city's Be A Smart Ash site are addressed to ash in parks, parkways, and the public right of way, and to property owners for their own trees.

## Finding 5: Size and condition, as recorded

Diameter is recorded as a text bin, not a measurement. The modal bin for ash is 12 to 18 inches (11,857 trees, 35.4 percent). The two smallest bins, 0 to 6 and 6 to 12 inches, hold 14,401 ash (43.0 percent); bins at 18 inches and above hold 7,163 (21.4 percent), and bins at 24 inches and above hold 2,505 (7.5 percent); 58 ash records carry no diameter bin. The city's Ash Removal and Replacement Program, as described on Be A Smart Ash, covers ash in the public right of way that are 12 inches or less in diameter and in declining or dead condition, among other conditions. The inventory's bins are ranges, so a tree recorded in the 6 to 12 bin cannot be placed exactly at or below 12 inches from this data alone; the 43.0 percent figure is the share in the two smallest recorded bins, not a program eligibility count.

Recorded condition for ash: Fair 21,188 (63.3 percent), Good 8,440 (25.2 percent), Poor 2,991 (8.9 percent), Very Poor 631 (1.9 percent), Dead 180 (0.5 percent), Excellent 24 (0.1 percent), and not recorded (N/A or blank) 25 (0.1 percent). Poor, very poor, and dead together are 3,802 ash (11.4 percent). For all other inventoried trees, the same three categories together are 10.7 percent. This brief does not attribute the difference between the ash and other-tree figures to emerald ash borer: the ratings are last-visit values spread over many years, and rating era and tree size are not controlled here. Condition is recorded at each tree's inventory date, which varies by record. For ash, the recorded inventory dates fall 60.2 percent in 2010 to 2015 (20,160 trees), 32.5 percent in 2016 to 2020 (10,894), and 7.2 percent in 2021 or later (2,425). Condition, diameter, and EAB fields in this brief are therefore the values from each tree's last inventory visit, not a 2026 survey.

## Finding 6: The inventory's own EAB field cannot say which ash are treated

The dataset carries an EAB_TAG field. For ash trees it is blank on 29,176 records (87.1 percent) and carries a value on 4,303. Across the full dataset the field holds 178 distinct values, including short codes (for ash: "xx" 1,733, "BB" 1,481, "TI" 545, "BB?" 67), the numeral "0" (329 ash records; 25,744 records dataset-wide, most of them not ash), stray numerals, and free-text notes. The dataset's published description does not define these values. This brief therefore reports no treatment count from EAB_TAG. Separately, the city's Be A Smart Ash site reports, as a city figure, that 6,000 ash trees have been treated in parks and parkways since the campaign began in 2016; that figure is the city's, reported here as reported, and it cannot be reconciled tree by tree against EAB_TAG from the published data.

## What this might mean

One reading of these figures is that ash exposure, measured on the city's own inventory, is uneven enough that a single citywide response pace would land very differently by neighborhood: Marston and Indian Creek hold roughly three times the citywide ash share, while the largest absolute counts sit in Gateway - Green Valley Ranch and Central Park. The strongest alternative explanation is that the inventory reflects where the city and adjacent owners planted heavily in past decades and where inventory coverage is most complete, so neighborhood shares partly record planting history and survey coverage rather than current canopy share. The inventory also cannot show which ash are already infested: it carries no EAB infestation field with defined values, and condition was recorded at varying inventory dates.

## Who can act

- **Office of the City Forester, Denver Parks and Recreation** controls the inventory and the ash programs. Concrete actions within its existing work: publish a codebook for EAB_TAG (or a defined treatment-status field) so treatment coverage can be counted from public data, and publish the inventory with untruncated location and site-designation values. Cost class: low-cost operational change. Outcome measure: the next inventory pull carries a defined EAB status field, and treated ash can be counted and compared with the 33,479 ash baseline in this brief.
- **Denver City Council**, in the 2027 budget cycle (hearing October 26, amendment vote November 2, passage November 9, per the Council's October 9, 2026 news release), controls forestry funding levels. Concrete action: set treatment, removal, and replacement volumes against the neighborhood distribution above, where Marston (29.6 percent ash) and Gateway - Green Valley Ranch (2,374 ash) present different problems, share versus count. Cost class: budget decision. Outcome measure: ash counts, condition distribution, and stump and vacant-site counts by neighborhood in the next inventory pull, against this brief's panel data file.
- **Owners of property adjacent to private-maintained street trees** hold 22,574 of the inventoried ash (67.4 percent) under that designation. Concrete action: identify ash on or beside their property and use the city's Be A Smart Ash guidance on treatment or replacement. Cost class: low-cost operational change for identification; treatment itself is a household expense, not estimated here. Outcome measure: the private-maintained street tree ash count and its condition distribution on inventory refresh.

## Data notes

- Inventory dates are recorded per record as text. The field carries 2 impossible values (1604-09-07 and 1899-12-29, both on vacant-site records) and no values after June 30, 2026; no date-range claim in this brief rests on the field's minimum or maximum.
- DIAMETER, CONDITION, EAB_TAG, SITE_DESIGNATION, LOCATION_NAME, and NEIGHBOR are all text fields as served; several values are truncated in the source at 28 characters (for example "Private Maintained Street Tr"). Figures use the values as filed.
- This brief is a snapshot analysis of one dataset. No dataset join was performed; neighborhood, location, and designation are fields carried in the source itself.
- Reproduction: code/pull_data.py records the server-side aggregate queries; code/audit_section10.py records the pre-flight audit (output in data/audit_output.txt); code/build_stats.py builds stats.json and data/neighborhood_panel_trees.csv; code/charts.py draws the charts.
